Dynamics, Guidance, and Control of a Low-Cost
Quadcopter-Based Space Vehicle Testbed
William J. Elke III∗ and Ryan J. Caverly†
University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, 55455
This paper presents the dynamic modeling, trajectory optimization, and control of the Cost-
and Risk-Reducing Quadcopter System (CRQS, pronounced “circus”). The CRQS is designed
to mature novel guidance and control algorithms for launch vehicle and planetary landing
systems. The components of the testbed—a quadcopter, a flexible inverted pendulum with an
end mass, and a hanging pendulum—are modeled as separate bodies, then constrained together
in the same manner as the physical prototype. An optimal trajectory is generated to softly
land the CRQS at a desired location based on the booster soft-landing algorithm presented
in existing literature. An inner-loop linear quadratic regulator with tracking is synthesized
to stabilize the CRQS and have it track the trajectory. Four different configurations of the
CRQS with progressively added features are numerically simulated. The results demonstrate
the CRQS’s ability to capture the dynamic features of launch vehicles and landing systems that
other platforms do not, which often need to be considered when synthesizing guidance and
control strategies.
I. Introduction
T
he National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) and companies such as Space Exploration Technologies
Corporation (SpaceX) are planning on sending humans to the Moon with the intent to send them to Mars
thereafter [1,2]. Specialized vehicles including, launch vehicles, spacecraft, and landing systems will be required to
carry the crew to their destinations. These mission vehicles are expensive, and since they will carry crew and critical
payloads, any failures are unacceptable. The technologies that make up the mission vehicles will need to become more
advanced in order to ensure crew and payload safety and mission success as the frequency and challenge of missions
increase. Due to the costs and risks associated with the mission vehicles, novel technologies must be rigorously tested
to prove that they will work without failure. Once the testing is complete, the technology will be considered “flight
proven.”
Consider the Technology Readiness Levels (TRLs) defined by NASA shown in Fig. 1. The TRLs are used as a
metric to measure how close to flight proven a novel technology is. Generally, TRLs 1–3 are characterized by the theory
and simulation to prove the concept of the technology. TRLs 4–6 are characterized by the low-cost and low-risk testing
in relevant/laboratory environments to validate the technology. TRLs 7–9 are characterized by the final testing required
to elevate the technology to flight proven. Although the TRLs were originally conceived for system-level technologies,
its straightforward how they can be tweaked to fit other applications such as software development [3]. Therefore, we
will use the TRL definitions here.
The technologies of interest for this work are the guidance and control (G&C) algorithms that operate on launch
and landing vehicles. These G&C algorithms are able to make it through TRLs 1–3, but often have to jump straight
to TRL 7. This is because the environments that they operate in are difficult to replicate in a laboratory or analogous
setting for TRLs 4–6 due to the inherent dynamics of the systems. These difficulties result in G&C algorithms with
extensive flight history to be favored over novel algorithms. A survey of expendable launch vehicle failures performed
in 2002 claimed that advanced G&C algorithms would have significantly reduce the failures and cost of failures of 2nd
generation reusable launch vehicles [4]. Since the survey, the claim has been substantiated—advanced G&C algorithms
have demonstrated their capacity to reduce the cost of failures and the cost of the overall mission. In August 2020,
for example, Astra LV0006 experienced a propellant leak on the launch pad which caused a mission failure and the
advanced G&C algorithm on board resulted in damage mitigation [5]. SpaceX has demonstrated the cost savings of the
overall mission through the now-famous vertical propulsive landing and reuse of their orbital rocket boosters—such
landings are impossible without advanced G&C algorithms [6].

A novel testbed for maturing guidance, navigation, and control technologies through TRLs 4–6 was proposed by the
authors in Ref. [10]. This testbed—known as the Cost- and Risk-Reducing Quadcopter System (CRQS, pronounced
“circus”)—is inspired by initial testbed concepts in Ref. [7–9] and is made up of a quadcopter with a flexible inverted
pendulumandahangingpendulum. Theinvertedpendulumallowsfortheaerodynamicinstabilityandthenon-minimum
phase behavior of a landing system to be considered. The structural dynamics and propellant slosh must be considered
in the control formulation for launch vehicles and landing systems, therefore, the testbed should include analogous
featurestocreatearelevantenvironment. Thus,theinclusionofaflexibleinvertedpendulumandahangingpendulumto
model the vehicle’s structural dynamics and fuel slosh is an important novel contribution of this testbed in comparison
to those that only model the launch-vehicle’s rigid-body dynamics in Ref. [16–18]. An image of a prototype of the
system that is currently being constructed at the University of Minnesota can be seen in Fig. 2.

end of the inverted pendulum and the hanging pendulum. The dynamic responses of the direct current (DC) motors that
provide control actuation of the quadcopter are approximated as first-order systems. An optimal trajectory is presented

III. Dynamic Modeling
The CRQS is modeled as separate (unconstrained) bodies representing the quadcopter, inverted pendulum, end mass
on the inverted pendulum, and hanging pendulum. The separate bodies are then constrained in place to behave like the
physical system using a multi-body modeling method that eliminates the Lagrange multipliers needed to enforce the
constraints. This method is described in Sec. 3.5.3 of Ref. [21] and Sec. 11.2.5 of Ref. [22] as well as applied to a
model of a kiteplane in Sec. III.D of Ref. [23]. In this method, the kinetic and potential energies of each body are first
derived, followed by the derivations of the generalized forces and moments acting on each body, the constraints, and the
equations of motion of the CRQS using a Lagrangian approach. A simplified dynamic model is then presented, which is
used with the trajectory optimization algorithm presented in Sec. V.B.
The reader can apply the modeling approach presented here to their own design of the CRQS should they want to
deviate from the configuration used in this paper. This is discussed at the end of each of the following subsections.

A. Trajectory Generation Algorithm
A trajectory is designed to softly land the CRQS from prescribed initial conditions while satisfying state and control
constraints. There are many approaches to generate a trajectory for the booster soft-landing algorithm (BSLA) [27–30],
but the one implemented here is the one developed in Ref. [19]. We chose this algorithm because it is relatively
straightforward to implement and a possible starting point for a practicing engineer. This is why a higher-fidelity
approach, which may employ successive convexification or additional state and control constraints [31, 32], is not
implemented here. Also, the goal of the CRQS is to help mature novel algorithms, and this algorithm is topical and
relatively novel.
TheBSLAiscompletedinthreesteps: 1)minimizedistancebetweenthelandinglocationandthetargetgivenatime
offlight(minimumlandingerrorproblem),2)minimizetheamountoffuelittakestogettothelandingsolutiontoStep1
given the same time of flight (minimum fuel problem), and 3) perform an optimizing line search over the time-of-flight.
These problems are originally non-convex due to the thrust magnitude and pointing constraints. Reference [19] applies
lossless convexification to make the problem convex, which results in a convex second-order cone program (SOCP).
What follows are the convexified versions of these problems (known as Problems 3 and 4 in Ref. [19]) adjusted for
implementation with the CRQS.

fuel. Theresultsofthisbehaviorareseenintheglideslopeangleandspeedconstraintplots. Theglideslopeangleateach
step increases between the minimum error and minimum fuel solutions, and the speed never reaches its limits, which we
expect to see in the minimum-time-of-flight solution. The feed-forward control commands,u 𝑓, are the constant DC
motor speeds that keep the quadcopter hovering, which are used in the linearization for the inner-loop control presented
in Sec. IV.B. Another approach could be to implementu 𝑓 (𝑡) as a function ofT𝑐 (𝑡) that corresponds tor∗ and v∗.
C. Trajectory Tracking Results & Discussion
Four simulations are performed in which a feature is successively added to the CRQS. The first simulation is of the
quadcopter and the rigid inverted pendulum (RIP). The second adds to this the hanging pendulum (RIP+HP), the third
addsthestructuraldynamicsoftheinvertedpendulum(i.e. flexiblependulum)andremovesthehangingpendulum(FIP),
and the fourth is of the entire system (FIP+HP). The weighting matrices used for the inner-loop LQR controller are
Q = diag{100 × 13×3, 15×5, 10 × 13×3, 15×5} and R = 1 × 10−4 × 14×4. Figure 8 includes plots of the quadcopter’s
position and velocity versus time for the three different simulations along with the trajectoryx𝑑 from Sec. V.B. Figure 9
presents the Euler 3-2-1 sequence of the quadcopter attitude computed from the DCM at each time step. Figure 10
includes plots of the inverted and hanging pendulums’ quadcopter-relative orientations as well as the tip deflection of
the inverted pendulum.
As expected, Figs. 8–10 show the tracking performance of the inner-loop controller degrades as additional features
are added with each simulation. When the hanging pendulum is added, low-frequency oscillations appear in the
responses. This is most apparent in the quadcopter attitude response of the RIP+HP configuration in Fig. 9, where the
additional low-frequency oscillations match the frequency of the quadcopter-relative hanging pendulum orientation
in Fig. 10. These oscillations are eventually damped out by the inner-loop controller, but that may not be the case if
the hanging pendulum is slightly different. Unstable high-frequency oscillations appear in the responses when the
structural dynamics are added. This is most noticeable in the quadcopter attitude response of the FIP and FIP+HP
configurations in Fig. 9 and the the tip deflection plots in Fig. 10. The addition of the structural dynamics drive the
system unstable because the closed-loop control system has a natural frequency near the first bending mode of the FIP.
To improve the trajectory tracking results, the unmodeled dynamics should be considered in the trajectory generation
algorithm and inner-loop controller synthesis. This highlights the need to consider fuel slosh and structural dynamics
when synthesizing a guidance and control strategy. Other testbeds, such as the ones in Ref. [16–18], consider the rigid

VI. Conclusion & Future Work
A dynamic model has been developed of a quadcopter-based test platform (known as the Cost- and Risk-Reducing
Quadcopter System) for maturing guidance, navigation, and control algorithms through the mid-level maturity levels.
An optimal trajectory was generated to softly land the CRQS based on the booster soft-landing algorithm presented in
Ref. [19]. An inner-loop LQR with tracking controller was synthesized with knowledge only of the quadcopter and a
rigid inverted pendulum. Results from simulations of four configurations of the CRQS highlighted the importance of
considering fuel slosh and structural dynamics when synthesizing a guidance and control strategy as well as the CRQS’s
usefulness as a platform for maturing G&C algorithms.
Futureworkwillincludeusingthesimulationto1)informthedesign,fabrication,andflightofthephysicalprototype,
and 2) validate more complex guidance and control algorithms. Although it is not addressed in this paper, the CRQS
may also be useful for validating novel navigation algorithms and hardware, which is another active area of research.

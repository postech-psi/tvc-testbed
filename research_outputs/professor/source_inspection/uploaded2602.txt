Abstract—While reinforcement learning (RL) has been suc-
cessfully applied to conventional quadrotors for agile and robust
flight, whether actuator-level RL can be reliably deployed on tilt-
rotor aerial robots remains an open question, as the hybrid actu-
ation coupling brushless rotors with rotational joints introduces
a substantially harder sim-to-real gap. In this work, we propose
an end-to-end RL framework for omnidirectional motion control
on overactuated tilt-rotor quadrotors, directly mapping target
poses to joint and rotor commands. The learning framework
combines actuator-level simulation with an asymmetric actor-
critic architecture for 6D pose-reaching. For reliable sim-to-
real transfer on the hybrid actuation, we integrate system
identification with minimal yet physically grounded domain
randomization. The trained policy is deployed zero-shot on
real hardware and evaluated across waypoint hovering, external
disturbances, payload variation and trajectory tracking, together
with simulated traversal of allocation-singular configurations.
The policy is compared with a state-of-the-art NMPC baseline:
NMPC attains lower steady-state position error, whereas the RL
policy offers a more uniform orientation error across evaluations,
transitions between poses faster, and requires less onboard

I. INTRODUCTION
Aerial robots have demonstrated remarkable capabilities
across a wide range of applications. In particular, quadrotors
have been extensively deployed in diverse real-world missions,
including inspection, delivery, and search and rescue [1].
Despite their success, conventional quadrotors are inherently
underactuated and cannot independently regulate position and
orientation, which limits their applicability to advanced aerial
interaction. To address this limitation, omnidirectional aerial
robots have recently attracted growing attention for their abil-
ity to perform full six-degree-of-freedom (6D) motion [2], [3],
[4]. Among the various designs, tilt-rotor platforms stand out
by actively reorienting individual thrust vectors, attaining high
actuation efficiency without internal force cancellation [4],
[5], [6]. However, this expanded capability comes at the
cost of substantially increased system complexity. The strong
coupling between joint and rotor dynamics, combined with
the highly flexible omnidirectional motion capability (Fig. 1),
poses fundamental challenges for motion control.
Model-based control has long been the mainstream ap-
proach for tilt-rotor aerial robots, with geometric control [4],
[7], [8] and Model Predictive Control (MPC) [9], [10] as
the most representative formulations. These methods typically
adopt a sequential structure, as illustrated in Fig. 2. For an
aerial robot expressed in the body frame with massm, inertia

Beyond accuracy and interpretability, this decomposition
keeps each subproblem solvable at the control rate, en-
abling onboard deployment. However, several characteristics
of model-based methods motivate complementary research
directions: (i) the model must stay tractable within the on-
line control period, so it is often reduced under simplifying
assumptions; unmodeled dynamics and external disturbances
[Fd τd]are then handled by dedicated observers and com-
pensated through the allocator [7], whereas learning-based
formulations can propagate the dynamic model offline, through
large-scale training-time exposure; (ii) the allocation matrix
Aα becomes ill-conditioned at certain joint configurations,
which prior work has addressed through explicit regularization
or actuator-dynamics integration [9], [11]. These observations
motivate us to investigate whether a policy trained offline can
directly operate actuators while subsuming planning, control,
and allocation within a single policy.
Learning-based approaches have emerged as a promising
alternative to model-based control and have showcased impres-
sive performance across various aerial robotics tasks. [12] pio-
neered actuator-level reinforcement learning (RL) for quadro-
tor waypoint tracking. [13] demonstrated robust disturbance
rejection under strong winds via deep learning combined with
meta-learning. [14] and [15] achieved aggressive quadrotor
flight comparable to human champions in drone racing. [16]
trained a single policy that generalizes across quadrotor plat-
forms with diverse physical parameters and rotor configura-
tions. While these studies highlight the potential of learning-
based control, they are largely confined to fixed-rotor plat-
forms. To the best of our knowledge, data-driven control for
tilt-rotor robots remains significantly underexplored, with only
a few works integrating learned components into MPC [17],
guiding policy training with a model-based action prior [18],
or fine-tuning actuator-level policies with real-world data for
sim-to-real transfer [19]. These preliminary efforts suggest that
learning-based control is promising for tilt-rotor platforms, but
also expose the substantial challenges in learning formulation
and sim-to-real transfer on such complex systems.
Adopting RL on a new robotic platform fundamentally re-
quires answering two questions: (1)whatlearning formulation
fits the platform, and (2)howcan the trained policy be reliably
transferred from simulation to the real system? Prior work on
fixed-rotor quadrotors has explored three action-space abstrac-
tions that occupy distinct points along the trade-off between
sim-to-real transferability and the breadth of system dynamics
they can exploit [20], as illustrated in Fig. 3. The Linear
Velocity (LV) abstraction is the most high-level: it transfers
easily with velocity controller but underexploits the platform’s
full dynamic capabilities. The Collective Thrust and Bodyrates
(CTBR) abstraction strikes a balance between transferability
and dynamic expressiveness, and is widely adopted on fixed-
rotor systems [15], [14]; however, applying CTBR to tilt-rotor
platforms still requires a low-level allocator beneath the policy,
leaving the singularity-prone allocation problem unresolved.
The Single-Rotor Thrusts (SRT) abstraction operates directly
at the actuator level and can holistically capture the platform’s
dynamics [12], but is less commonly used because of the
stronger sim-to-real demands it imposes.

Given the highly flexible motion capability and strong joint-
rotor coupling of tilt-rotor platforms, we adopt an actuator-
level abstraction analogous to SRT and propose an end-to-end
RL framework for 6D pose-reaching control on overactuated
tilt-rotor quadrotors. The policy directly maps the target pose
and current state to joint position and rotor thrust commands
with a single policy. However, this approach introduces a
more severe sim-to-real gap, further exacerbated by the hybrid
actuation system that couples brushless rotors with servo-
driven joints. To bridge this gap, we integrate actuator system
identification with minimal yet physically grounded domain
randomization into a sim-to-real pipeline, validated through
extensive real-world experiments. The main contributions are
summarized as follows:
•An end-to-end reinforcement learning framework is pro-
posed, which produces joint and rotor commands from
a single policy on overactuated tilt-rotor quadrotors, en-
abling actuator-level 6D pose-reaching control.
•A sim-to-real pipeline tailored to the hybrid actuation
is introduced, integrating actuator system identification,
control-architecture latency alignment, and minimal yet
physically grounded domain randomization.
•Zero-shot deployment across five real-world flight scenar-
ios and a simulated traversal of allocation-singular con-
figurations, together with a matched comparison against
a servo-integrated NMPC baseline on the same platform
and software stack, in which the policy holds a more
uniform orientation error at lower online computation.

II. METHOD
The proposed RL framework for pose-reaching control is
designed in accordance with the real robot platform and
deployment scheme, as shown in Fig. 4.
A. Preliminaries
1) Platform Introduction:The tilt-rotor quadrotor platform
consists of four rotor modules, each mounted at the tip of
one arm. Each module uses a three-bladed9 inpropeller
driven by a T-Motor AT2814 KV900 brushless DC motor
and is connected to the arm through a single-degree-of-
freedom rotational joint actuated by a DYNAMIXEL XC330
T181-T servomotor. This design enables full 6-DOF wrench
generation; the detailed physical parameters are listed in
Appendix IV-A.

C. Sim-to-Real Transfer
1) System Latency:System latency is unavoidable on real-
world platforms and can degrade control performance or even
induce divergent oscillations. To bridge this gap, we explicitly
model the dominant delays during training: the commanded
q∗

actuation delay, and the corresponding joint feedbackq j,t is
returned with a sensing delay. The base velocitiesv b,t and

timator, which fuses motion-capture and IMU measurements.
These delays are estimated by collecting real platform data.
2) Rotor Model:The rotor module serves as the primary
actuator of the aerial robot. Since rotor thrust dynamics are

identify the rotor thrust and reaction torque empirically by
sweeping PWM commands and supply voltages on a 6-axis
force-torque sensor, and fit polynomial functions to map the

4) Domain Randomization and Noise:Domain randomiza-
tion (DR) is widely adopted to improve policy robustness
and facilitate sim-to-real transfer, but excessive randomization
trades off task-level performance [25]. We therefore apply
only minimal and physically grounded randomization to the
body mass, body inertia, and joint position offsets, while
injecting observation noise to account for sensor uncertainties.
Combined with the actuator-level system identification of
Sec. II-C2 and Sec. II-C3, this conservative scheme keeps the
training environment closely aligned with the real platform
while preserving robustness. The details of the proposed sim-

III. EXPERIMENTS
A. Experimental Setup
The training environment is built on Isaac Sim [26] and
Isaac Lab [27], and the policy is optimized with proximal
policy optimization (PPO) [28] using the RSL-RL implemen-
tation [29]. Training is performed on a single NVIDIA A10
GPU, consuming approximately16 hfor 20,000 iterations;
full hyperparameters are listed in Appendix IV-B. The learned
policy is then deployed zero-shot in both simulation and
on the real platform, without any fine-tuning. To facilitate
reproducibility, the training and deployment code is publicly
available at github.com/ut-dragon-lab/aerial lab.
As a baseline, we reuse the servo-integrated NMPC of [9],
running at100 Hzon the onboard computer and fully inte-
grated into the robot software stack as the control module
analogous to the actor network in Fig. 4. Its prediction model
combines single-rigid-body dynamics with a first-order servo
model over a horizon of2 s(20 steps), and shares the thrust
mapping of Sec. II-C2. The NMPC parameters are fine-tuned
for our platform and held fixed across the different scenarios.

IV. CONCLUSION
This paper presented a sim-to-real reinforcement learning
pipeline forSE(3)pose-reaching on overactuated tilt-rotor
quadrotors. A single policy directly produces joint and rotor
commands for zero-shot real-world deployment with actuator-
level system identification, latency alignment, and physically
grounded domain randomization to bridge the gap induced
by the hybrid joint-rotor actuation. Experiments demonstrate
consistent performance across waypoint hovering, external dis-
turbances, payload variation, trajectory tracking, and continu-
ous rotation through allocation-singular configurations, the last
typically requiring explicit regularization in allocation-based
controllers. As the dynamics calculation is performed during
training, the policy keeps its online computation minimal,

=== PDF PAGE 8 ===
8
which suits platforms with limited onboard resources and tasks
that demand consistent behavior across conditions rather than
absolute accuracy. Two limitations remain: the platform model
omits aerodynamic effects such as wind drag and inter-rotor
downwash, calling for richer real-to-sim modeling; and the
reward relies on manually shaped terms to coordinate the
joint-rotor dynamics, motivating more principled formulations.
Further hardware optimization and control problem formula-
tion are needed to fully unlock the potential of actuator-level
policies on tilt-rotor platforms.

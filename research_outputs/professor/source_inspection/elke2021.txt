Abstract—This research seeks to bridge the gap between the sim-
ulation/theory and the ﬂight-testing phases of control algorithm
design for launch vehicles (LVs) and landing systems (LSs). This
paper reviews contemporary testbeds for LV and LS control sys-
tem design with an emphasis on reducing costs and risks. Also, a
new low-cost and low-risk testbed utilizing a quadcopter, ﬂexible
inverted pendulum, and a hanging pendulum is proposed. It is
shown that the planar dynamic response of this proposed testbed
can match the planar dynamic response of an LV .
As mission objectives for frequent cislunar and interplanetary
travel become more complex, the algorithms that control LVs
and LSs need to become more advanced to ensure mission
safety and success. These control systems are slow to mature
because it is difﬁcult to perform experiments in an environment
relevant to the mission in a safe and cost-effective manner. A
solution to this problem frequently leveraged in the aviation and
satellite industries involves mimicking the dynamic response of
the vehicle with a test platform that is low-cost and low-risk.

1. I NTRODUCTION
In recent years, the interest in regular cislunar and inter-
planetary travel has increased [1, 2]. As these missions
progress, their objectives will increase in complexity. This
then requires that the technology used to accomplish these ob-
jectives becomes more advanced. In particular, the Guidance,
Navigation, and Control (GN&C) systems on vehicles used
to deliver payloads to their destinations need to become more
advanced in order to ensure mission completion and safety of
the payload. The work described in this paper deals with the
control systems used on launch vehicles (LVs) and landing

systems (LSs), with an emphasis on methods to efﬁciently
develop and mature advanced control system concepts.
Motivation
A review of LV failures performed in 2002 highlights the
beneﬁts of utilizing advanced GN&C systems on LVs. The
review found that 41% of all LV failures between 1990 and
2002 could have been prevented or mitigated had an advanced
GN&C system been used [3]. In September 2020, an LV
built by Astra Space crashed 30 seconds after liftoff due to
roll oscillations caused by the guidance software forcing the
launch controllers to command an engine shutdown for safety
reasons [4]. If an advanced control system had been used,
the damage could have potentially been mitigated while still
maintaining the same safety standards.
Autonomously landing on another celestial body is challeng-
ing. Extensive research and design went into the GN&C
systems used on the Apollo system, which resulted in only
one failure of the primary guidance system in all of its
manned missions [5, 6]. Despite this success, Apollo 11
and the ﬁve successive lunar landings were manually piloted
because the astronauts felt more comfortable piloting the
vehicle themselves. This can be partially attributed to limita-
tions in the autonomous GN&C technology. Also, consider
the Chandrayaan-2 mission operated by the Indian Space
Research Organization (ISRO). The lander (called Vikram)
that ﬂew with the mission in late 2019 lost communication
during the last 15 minutes of descent and later was found to
have hard landed onto the lunar surface. One of the possible
causes for the failure is a lack of robustness in the control
system design [7]. This shows that as planetary landing
missions become more complex, it is necessary to have an
advanced control system to ensure success.
In this paper, the term “advanced control systems” refers to
control systems that extend beyond the classical proportional-
integral-derivative control and the use of gain and phase
stability margins as a measure of robustness. Classical control
methods have a lengthy history in the control design of
space vehicles; control engineers typically select them for
their legacy and easy quantiﬁcation of robustness through
stability margins. Advanced control systems for use on LVs
and LSs have difﬁculties reaching the maturity required due
to a lack in legacy, testing, and extensive theory behind
frequency domain analysis. One metric that can be used
to describe the maturity of a technology is known as the

Technology Readiness Levels (TRLs), as deﬁned by the
National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) and

systems often have difﬁculties progressing past TRLs 4-6; the
so-called “technology valley of death”.
Generally, full-scale ﬂight testing is required to advance the
TRL beyond 6. However, these undertakings are expensive
and risky. To bridge the gap between simulation and full-
scale ﬂight testing, aviation and spacecraft industries often
use low-cost and low-risk (LCR) testbeds as an intermediate
step to further validate the design and mitigate potential risks.
Prior Art
The aviation industry has utilized many LCR testbeds to
simulate various aerodynamic effects and to design control
systems for full-scale vehicles. The Airborne Subscale
Transport Aircraft Research (AirSTAR) project at NASA
Langley Research Center (LaRC) uses subscale aircraft to
test GN&C technologies aimed at reducing loss-of-control
accidents in a environment [8]. The AirSTAR infrastructure
has even been used recently to test the novel control scheme
of a robust linear controller with composite adaptive control
augmentation [9]. The X-48B aircraft at NASA Armstrong
Flight Research Center (AFRC) was an 8.5%-scale low-
speed vehicle that saw success in the design and testing of a
preliminary control system for the full-scale Blended Wing
Body type aircraft [10]. A number of testbeds have been
built for researching vehicles with high-aspect-ratio wings.
This type of vehicle can experience aeroelastic ﬂutter past a
certain airspeed, which can cause devasting damage to the
airframe in ﬂight if not accounted for carefully. Examples
of testbeds that study this effect include Lockheed Martin’s
Body Freedom Flutter vehicle [11], NASA’s X-56 Multi Util-
ity Technology Testbed (MUTT) aircraft [12], and University
of Minnesota’s mini MUTT [13] and mAEWing aircraft [14].
All of these vehicles feature various control techniques to
suppress the aeroelatic ﬂutter. A variety of ﬂying unmanned
aerial vehicles and autonomous cars are part of an LCR
testbed at Purdue University that is used for researching the
challenges faced by full-scale autonomous vehicle systems
used for applications such as urban air mobility, wildﬁre
suppression, and military missions among others [15]. NASA
AFRC has been at the forefront of ﬂight control research for
decades through the use of F/A-18 jets for research purposes.
Some areas of research include the effects of high angle-of-
attack, autonomous formation ﬂight, and airborne refueling;
most notably for the focus of this paper, these aircraft have
been used for researching a wide range of advanced control
techniques [16].
The spacecraft industry has also utilized LCR testbeds when
designing and validating GN&C systems. The two primary
methods being: ﬂight testing with CubeSats and ground-
based facilities. By design, CubeSats are meant to be low-
cost and low-risk. As such, they are used in a wide range
of applications ranging from biomedical to Earth Science
applications [17]. Some of the notable CubeSat missions
include demonstrating GN&C functionalities for the chal-
lenging task of precise formation ﬂying in low Earth orbit.
In 2014, the CanX −4 and CanX−5 CubeSats performed
a series of precise formation ﬂying experiments after sys-
tematically eliminating large initial differences in the orbital
elements [18]. Around the same time, the CubeSat Proximity
Operations Demonstration mission was initiated. This mis-
sion is intended to demonstrate key rendezvous, proximity
operation, and docking technologies [19, 20]. Ground-based
testbeds offer an even cheaper alternative to ﬂight tests with
CubeSats. These testbeds use air bearings and are relatively
accurate in the portrayal of planar dynamics in a low-gravity
environment. Some early examples of these include the air
spindle and triaxial air bearing testbeds built at the University
of Michigan and a low-cost spacecraft simulator at the Geor-
gia Institute of Technolgy in the early 2000s [21, 22]. More
recently, the Spacecraft Attitude Testbed was built at San
Diego State University [23]. While these testbeds are low-
cost and low-risk, their usefulness beyond designing attitude
control systems is limited due to their stationary nature. More
advanced testbeds allow for translation and rotation; these 3
degree-of-freedom (DOF) facilities are useful for simulating
close-proximity operations and docking of spacecrafts while
in space [24, 25].
The way to maturing low-TRL advanced control systems for
LVs and LSs is through LCR testbeds. However, there is a
conspicuous dearth of LCR testbeds for LVs and LSs. This
is, in part, because there are some very difﬁcult challenges
associated with replicating the conditions for LVs and LSs.
Paper Contribution
The purpose this paper is to explore the state of the art in
LCR testbeds for LVs and LSs. It is also to propose a concept
for such an LCR testbed and compare its dynamic similarities
with an LV . Future work addressing the technical challenges
associated with the design is discussed.
The remainder of the paper progresses as follows: Section
2 reviews contemporary testbeds that have been used for
control design for LVs and LSs. Section 3 proposes a new
LCR testbed that can be used for control design for LVs and
LSs and evaluates its dynamics similitude to those systems.
Section 4 provides some concluding remarks on the results of
this paper and future work.

2. L ITERATURE REVIEW
A review of testbeds used for the design and testing of ﬂight
controllers for LVs and LSs is presented in this section.
Launch Vehicle Testbeds
This section encompasses a review of testbeds utilized for the
development of control systems for expendable LVs during
ascent and reusable LVs during ascent and descent back to
Earth. This section reviews the testbeds used by SpaceX,
Blue Origin, the European Space Agency (ESA), and NASA.
To the best of the knowledge of the authors, SpaceX does
not have any documented LCR testbeds for testing the ﬂight
controllers used for their reusable LVs during ascent or
descent. The company does, however, perform tests using
vehicles that are reduced cost and risk compared to the actual
ﬂight vehicle; speciﬁcally for the descent and landing of the
reusable LVs. The approach involves incrementally designing
and testing the vehicle in steps. This starts with a vehicle that
is as low-cost and low-risk as possible and performing a basic
functionality test [26]. Each subsequent step then slightly
advances the system towards the full vehicle capabilities by
expanding upon the test objectives. For the development of
the Falcon 9 (F9) and Falcon Heavy LVs, SpaceX constructed
two experimental reusable LVs named Grasshopper and the
Falcon 9 Reusable Development Vehicle (F9R Dev). These
test articles were intended to certify key ﬂight hardware and
GN&C algorithms [27, 28]. The eight ﬂights of Grasshopper
and ﬁve ﬂights of the F9R Dev contributed immensely to the
success of the F9 vehicle [29].
Blue Origin takes a similar incremental approach in certifying
its LVs. Since its ﬁrst mission in 2007, the company has stood
by their belief “in incremental improvement and in keeping
investments at a pace that is sustainable” [30]. Although these
incremental tests are lower cost and risk compared to the full
ﬂight-ready vehicle, these testbeds still remain far too costly
and risky for simply validating novel GN&C algorithms.
During a ﬂight of Blue Origin’s second test vehicle, “a ﬂight
instability drove an angle of attack that triggered the range
safety system to terminate thrust on the vehicle” [31]. An
advanced control system could have potentially been able to
account for this anomaly and saved the mission.
ESA launched the Future Launchers Preparatory Programme
in 2003 in order to advance launcher technologies aimed
at improving performance and reliability. One area of fo-
cus of the program is the reusability of existing and new
technologies including reusable rocket stages. In order to
design and test the GN&C systems of the reusable boosters,
the Demonstrator Technology Vehicle (DTV) seen in Fig-
ure 2 was developed and is currently undergoing qualiﬁcation
testing. This vehicle is an LCR platform which will allow
for ﬂight demonstrations of GN&C technologies. Recently,
the DTV was ﬂight tested in Romania in October 2020 and
a larger version of the vehicle with more engines is under
consideration [32]. As with the Blue Origin experimental
test vehicles, the DTV is still far too risky to use strictly
for validating GN&C algorithms despite the relative low cost
compared to an actual ﬂight vehicle.
NASA AFRC has been at the cutting edge of ﬂight controls
research with their use of modiﬁed F/A-18 jets for a wide
range of experiments. One of these highly modiﬁed F/A-18
jets named the Full-Scale Advanced Systems Testbed (FAST)
was modiﬁed in such a way to be able to research launch
vehicle adaptive controllers and can be seen in Figure 3.

The FAST is an aircraft, not an LV , and therefore, there are
inherent limitations. In addition, an F/A-18 jet with a human
pilot contains inherent costs and risks associated with each
test ﬂight that cannot be removed.
While FAST was used to validate the SLS AAC algorithm,
simultaneous efforts at NASA LaRC were performed for
further validation. These validation experiments were per-

the fundamental dynamics of the vehicle. These dynamics
include: open-loop instability caused by the aerodynamic
side-force, fuel slosh dynamics, structural bending dynamics,
and analogous control authority. The ﬁrst of the LCR testbeds
used was a 2-DOF pole-cart platform shown in Figure 4(a).
This testbed involved a cart moving along a track in order
to balance an inverted pendulum. The open-loop instability

Landing System Testbed Review
This section reviews platforms designed to test GN&C sys-
tems for vehicles with the objective of landing softly on
celestial bodies. LS testbeds can be classiﬁed into three types
at various levels of complexity: Pilot-in-the-Loop (PIL),
Vertical Testbeds (VTBs), and low-cost-and-risk.
The class of PIL test platforms are characterized by their
purpose of training a human pilot to control an LS. The two
Lunar Landing Research Vehicles (LLRVs) and three Lunar
Landing Training Vehicles (LLTVs) were used in the 1960s
to train astronauts of the Apollo program and are currently the
only testbed in the class of PIL test platforms. These vehicles
were speciﬁcally designed to mimic lunar landings, therefore,
they needed to simulate the lunar environment as closely as
possible. This requirement led to the design of the center-
mounted gimbaled engine used to compensate for the gravity
Figure 5: The Lunar Landing Research Vehicle (LLRV)
#1 in ﬂight in 1964. Photo: NASA.
difference between the Earth and the Moon. The testbed
utilized hydrogen-peroxide thrusters for attitude actuation,
and the pilot’s cockpit was located on the vehicles as demon-
strated in Figure 5 [38]. The LLRVs/LLTVs were difﬁcult
to pilot, risky, and expensive. Three of the ﬁve vehicles
crashed before the end of the Apollo program. Despite this,
the astronauts unanimously agreed that the training on the
LLRVs/LLTVs prepared them for the actual missions [39].
Although the PIL class of testbeds may only contain the
LLRVs and LLTVs currently, it will become more populated
in the future to train pilots for crewed lunar landings and
beyond. PIL test platforms can potentially be used to design
novel LS controllers, especially for manual control consid-
erations. However, these vehicles are inherently high-risk
due to the risks associated with an onboard pilot during ﬂight
testing.
In the past decade, NASA has incorporated rocket propelled
testbeds to test novel guidance and navigation systems. These
testbeds are vertical take-off and landing (VTOL); hence-
forth, these testbeds will be referred to as vertical testbeds
(VTBs).
A collaboration between Johns Hopkins Applied Physics
Laboratory and NASA Marshall Space Flight Center pro-
duced a VTB named the Mighty Eagle (Figure 6) with
assistance from commercial partners. The objectives of the
Mighty Eagle were to test and mature the GN&C system for
the ﬁnal 30-50 m (98-164 ft) of the descent stage before land-
ing with an emphasis on the optical navigation system [40].
This testbed had 20 successful ﬂights during its 2011 and
2012 outdoor ﬂight test campaigns in which it tested the
optical navigation systems used for autonomous rendezvous
and capture as well as other guidance and navigation func-
tionalities [40].
The Morpheus VTB (Figure 7) tested guidance and navi-
gation sensors for the Autonomous precision Landing and
Hazard Avoidance Technology (ALHAT) project [41]. After
Morpheus, NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory worked with
Masten Space Systems (MSS) to build the Autonomous
Descent and Ascent Powered-Flight Testbed (ADAPT). The
MSS Xombie vehicle was used to test terrain-relative nav-
igation and fuel-optimal onboard planning of large divert
trajectories from ADAPT [42, 43]. Subsequently, a payload
called COBALT was designed to ﬂy on the MSS Xodiac
vehicle (Figure 8). Flying COBALT on Xodiac allowed

Research Vehicle (ESLRV) and the Environment for Au-

The ESLRV was designed in 2010 to replicate the beneﬁts
of the LLTV while eliminating certain risks involved during
LLTV ﬂight tests. The ESLRV is a scaled down and less
expensive version of the LLTV and could be piloted remotely.
Like the LLTV , it utilizes a vertically-mounted, gimballed
jet engine to simulate lunar gravity. It uses four propellers
mounted like a quadcopter for attitude control instead of
using hydrogen peroxide thrusters [39]. Other improvements
made to the system came from using contemporary technol-
ogy. A full design and development breakdown of the ESLRV
can be found in [47]. The major drawback is that the jet
engine has inherent costs and risks.
The EAGLE was designed for control system design for LSs
as well as to mature other GN&C technologies. This testbed
utilizes a thrust-vector-controlled jet engine with cold gas
thrusters that makes this system comparable to an LV or LS.
This testbed also contains liquid fuel that would capture the
effect of the slosh dynamics onboard an LV or LS. A full
design and development breakdown of the EAGLE can be
found in [48]. Similar to the ESLRV , the major drawback to
this design is the risk and cost associated with the jet engine.
Summary
Simulation and theory alone can bring the TRL of an ad-
vanced control algorithm for an LV or LS to a 4 or 5. To
advance the TRL to 6 or beyond, experimental ﬂight testing is
required. This, however, comes with its own risks and often
the cost is upwards of millions of dollars. As illustrated in
Figure 9, there is currently a lack of LCR testbeds for matur-
ing mid-TRL ﬂight control algorithms. In this research, we
seek a high-ﬁdelity LCR testbed to bridge this gap between
simulation and ﬂight testing. This work builds off the work
described in [34–36]. The proposed testbed is described in
Section 3.

3. P ROPOSED TESTBED
A brief description of the proposed LCR testbed for LV
and LS control system design is described in this section.
Additionally, the dynamics of an LV and the proposed testbed
are described.
Setup
The intent of the proposed testbed is not necessarily to have
an exact one-to-one representation of the dynamics of a
particular LV or LS conﬁguration, but rather to be able to
capture the fundamental dynamics of these systems such that
it can be used to improve the TRL of novel control algorithms.
For an LV , the testbed must have the capability to char-
acterize the following fundamental dynamics: aerodynamic
instability, structural bending (ﬂexible) dynamics, and fuel
slosh [49–51]. LVs are typically aerodynamically unstable
due to the center of pressure being ahead of the center of
mass as shown in Figure 10. Also, the vehicle itself is
not a perfect rigid body thus susceptible to control-structure
interactions. The ﬁrst bending mode of a typical LV is
illustrated in Figure 10. Extensive literature exists on me-
chanical models for fuel slosh and their impact on ﬂight
control design [52–54]. The described fundamental dynamics
of an LV share similarities with the dynamics of an LS, which
are described in [38, 55–57].
A schematic of the proposed testbed is shown in Figure 11.
The testbed consists of a quadrotor helicopter (quadcopter)
with a ﬂexible inverted pendulum pinned to the top of the
vehicle and a hanging pendulum pinned to the bottom. Ve-
hicle aerodynamic instability can be simulated through the
inherently unstable dynamics of the inverted pendulum. The
ﬂexible dynamic effects are mimicked via the ﬂexibility of the
inverted pendulum. Furthermore, the degree of aerodynamic
instability and natural frequencies of the bending modes can
be adjusted by varying the length of the inverted pendulum.
The control effectors of the LV or LS are represented by the
quadcopter rotors; the quadcopter allocates speed commands
to each rotor for attitude and throttle control similar to a
thrust vector control system. Lastly, the fuel-slosh dynam-
ics are mimicked by the hanging pendulum. Recognizable
limitations of the testbed include: not being able to simulate
a vehicle conﬁguration with multiple propellant tanks, slosh
damping via bafﬂes, localized paneling bending, tail-wags-
dog, and dog-wags-tail effects [58].

Analysis
Following the derivation of the linear equations of motion
for the two systems, the poles of the open-loop plants are
compared in an effort to identify similarities in the dynamic
responses. Figure 14 provides an illustration of the funda-
mental dynamics of a typical LV and the quadcopter testbed.
Only the ﬁrst bending modes and a single fuel tank are
considered for this analysis. Table 1 and Table 2 (both located
at the end of the appendix) contain the values of the LV and
testbed parameters respectively. The testbed has two extra
poles both located at s = 0, representing the neutral stability
of the quadcopter attitude dynamics. The poles located at
s =±0.62 correspond to the aerodynamic instability of the
LV and the gravitational instability of the inverted pendulum.
The poles at s =−0.06± 12.5j correspond to the ﬂexible
dynamics of the LV and the inverted pendulum. The poles
located at s ≈ ±3.1j correspond to the dynamics of the
fuel slosh and hanging pendulum. Note in Figure 14 some
unrealistic parameters are chosen for the testbed to exactly
match the dynamics of the LV . In practice, the choice of these
parameters will be largely limited by operational constraints.
4. C ONCLUSIONS & F UTURE WORK
This paper surveyed the literature for LCR testbeds used
to mature ﬂight controllers for LVs and LSs. Despite the

numerous potential beneﬁts, it appears that there is a lack
of such testbeds applicable to TRLs 4-6 across academia,
